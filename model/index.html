    Model Description

    The purpose of this package is to unify multiple variants of the impulse-response model under a single, modular, Bayesian framework, and to resolve some of its statistical flaws by sharing information across different metrics and by mitigating ill-conditioned parameter spaces with physiologically meaningful restricted priors.

    Background

    The Banister impulse-response model (and its many variants) provides insights into the effects of training on performance through the inference of athetic performance's responses to training dose as well as the prediction of future performance. It suffers from two interacting challenges: performance data are expensive and physically demanding to collect and therefore sparsely observed, and the parameters of interest are ill-conditioned. Without a dense, high-signal dataset from which to learn, actionable insights from posterior parameter estimates and performance forecasts are unlikely.

    The original model

    The original Banister 1975, Calvert 1976 model is defined by

    \[p_t = p_0 + k_F\sum_{s=0}^{t-1} w_s e^{-(t-s)/\tau_F} - k_G\sum_{s=0}^{t-1} w_s e^{-(t-s)/\tau_G}\]

    where \(p_0\) is an initial performance value and \(p_t\) and \(w_t\) are performance and training load at time \(t\), respectively. The first and second summands above are commonly called "fitness" and "fatigue" and are described by their gain parameters \(k_F, k_G\) and decay rates \(\tau_F, \tau_G\).

    Statistical flaws in the original model

    Hellard 2006 first quantified the ill-conditioning issue. Using nine elite swimmers with an average of 13 performances across a single training season, it was demonstrated that, even though goodness-of-fit was strong (average \(R^2 \approx 0.79\)), the uncertainty in the parameter estimates was wide (the bootstrapped coefficient of variation was over 30% for the decay parameters and 60% for the gain parameters). This ill-conditioning was primarily caused by correlation of the posterior estimates (0.91 for the gain terms and 0.99 for the decay rates). In other words, there is a large set of widely varying parameter combinations that give similar model errors. It isn't difficult to see why: fitness and fatigue are antagonistic terms competing to explain changes in sparsely observed performance using similar time-filters on the same training load variable.

    Vermeire 2022 reproduced Hellard's result with eight performance observations on a cycle ergometer from a single subject and visualized the model error over a grid of parameter combinations, further demonstrating that (a) the error landscape over \(\tau_F\) and \(\tau_G\) is characterized by a sharp ridge near \(\tau_F = \tau_G\), and (b) the physiologically sensible region in which incremental fitness gains are smaller than fatigue gains, \(k_F < k_G\), results in consistently lower error than the unreasonable alternative.

    Peng 2024 built the first Bayesian Banister model. Importantly, they use informative prior parameterizations to provide a prior belief about values the parameters can take based on existing results from the literature. Importantly, they impose an informative prior structure by reparameterizing \(k_G = \theta k_F\) and imposing the constraint \(\theta > 1\); in other words, preventing the model from considering the physiologically unreasonable region \(k_F > k_G\) that Vermeire demonstrated resulted in poor model fit. They demonstrate that the Bayesian approach resulted in better parameter estimates, though uncertainty was still large, likely due to fitting the model to only 13 performance measurements (with additional noise likely caused by each performance being races of different lengths, and by imputing some missing training loads).

    Marchal 2025 also take a Bayesian approach and test weakly- versus moderately-informed Bayesian priors. However, they drop the physiological constraint \(\theta > 1\) and use performance measurements from short-track speed skaters previously demonstrated to be low-signal (average \(R^2 \approx 0.20\)) via alternative machine learning methods. Furthermore, their MCMC traceplots demonstrate a single stuck chain, which may be indicative of a poorly tuned sampler rather than model issues. They conclude that the impulse response model presents major dysfunctions, though their conclusion rests on an estimate of a posterior distribution that did not converge, and on cross-validation applied to a small set of performance measurements (roughly 35 performances from nine athletes, of which roughly ten performances are allocated to the testing dataset).

    The BIRM Prior

    The ill-conditioning issue is mitigated with a reparameterization. BIRM adopts Peng's constraint \(k_F < k_G\) to restrict the model to only search for optimal parameter combinations in a physiologically reasonable space. To motivate similar reparameterizations for the decay terms, we represent \(k_G/k_F\) in terms of decay rates and the time it takes the impulse response to recover \(t_r\) and peak \(t_p\):

    \[k_G/k_F = (\tau_F/\tau_G)^{\left(\frac{1}{t_p/t_r - 1}\right)} \]

    This satisfies the constraint \(k_G/k_F > 1\) either when \(\tau_F > \tau_G\) and \(t_p > t_r\), or when \(\tau_F < \tau_G\) and \(t_p < t_r\). The former is the physiologically reasonable scenario: fitness is accumulated over longer timescales than fatigue, and the impulse response recovers before it peaks.

    BIRM therefore uses the following informed parameterization by default:

    \[k_G = (\phi + 1) k_F \quad \text{ and } \quad \tau_F = 1 + \tau_G + \lambda_F,\]
    \[\phi \sim \text{LogNormal}(\mu_\phi, \sigma_\phi^2),\]
    \[\lambda_F \sim \text{LogNormal}(\mu_{\lambda_F}, \sigma_{\lambda_F}^2)\]

    which produces posterior estimates that satisfy the constraints \(k_G/k_F > 1\) and \(\tau_F > \tau_G + 1\) with certainty. The one-day offset keeps the sampler off the singular \(\tau_F = \tau_G\) ridge. The direct parameterization instead samples \(k_F, k_G, \tau_F, \tau_G\) independently from weakly-informative priors; it is the ill-conditioned baseline that informed is designed to fix.

    Multivariate Inputs and Outputs

    Assimilating additional data alongside performance (e.g., heart rate variability from consumer wearables) and controlling for performance effect modifiers (e.g., temperature and other external stressors) can further refine parameter estimates and improve forecast performance. To set notation, let \(w_t\) be training load, \(f_t\) be fitness, \(g_t\) be fatigue, and \(R_t = f_t - g_t\) be the impulse response on day \(t\). Multiple target variables (e.g., HRV and speed) can be represented as transformed, noisy measurements of the underlying IR model. The \(j^{th}\) response variable \(j=1,...,J\) at time \(t\), \(Y^{(j)}_t\), is given by

    \[ Y^{(j)}_t = \alpha^{(j)} + \gamma^{(j)} l^{(j)}\left(R_t\right) + X^{(j)}_t\beta^{(j)} + u^{(j)}_t + \epsilon^{(j)}_t\]

    where \(\alpha^{(j)}\) is the intercept analogous to initial performance \(p_0\) in Banister's formulation, \(\gamma^{(j)}\) is a regression parameter for the linear relationship between the measurement and the transformed latent impulse response model, \(l^{(j)}(\cdot)\) is a link function to represent nonlinear relationships across measurement types, and \(\beta^{(j)}\) are coefficients corresponding to exogenous predictors \(X^{(j)}\).

    Residuals are composed into two terms: \(\epsilon^{(j)}_t \sim N(0, \sigma^2_\epsilon)\) representing IID noise, and \(u^{(j)}_t\), a lag-1 autoregressive process orthogonalized to lie in the null space of the intercept and the fitness and fatigue integrals and capturing variation (e.g., slow drift due to unmeasured stress and fatigue, daily responses to sleep and nutrition, etc.) not explained by the main effects.

    In this package, every term except the impulse-response \(R_t\) and the white noise \(\epsilon^{(j)}_t\) is optional. Note that there are \(J+1\) possible time grids; they are "ragged" in that training loads \(w_t\) and channels \(Y^{(j)}_t\) exist on their own measurement cadence. Each day may or may not have an observation from each channel, independently.

    Modifications to the impulse-response model

    The original Banister 1975 IR model is defined by

    \[R^{(O)}_t = k_F\sum_{s=0}^{t-1} w_s e^{-(t-s)/\tau_F} - k_G\sum_{s=0}^{t-1} w_s e^{-(t-s)/\tau_G}\]

    Busso 2003 modified the fatigue gain to vary over time by responding to training load:

    \[R^{(V)}_t = k_F\sum_{s=0}^{t-1} w_s e^{-(t-s)/\tau_F} - \sum_{s=0}^{t-1}k_G(s) w_s e^{-(t-s)/\tau_G},\]
    \[k_G(t) = \sum_{s=0}^{t-1} w_s e^{-(t-s)/\tau_v}.\]

    The nonlinear fatigue gain accumulates with more recent training on timescales that are expected to be smaller than fitness and fatigue level. We therefore impose the physiological constraint \(\tau_F > \tau_G > \tau_v\) with the reparameterization \(\tau_G = \tau_v + \lambda_G\) and \(\tau_F = 1 + \tau_G + \lambda_F\) with log-normal priors on \(\lambda_G\) and \(\lambda_F\).

    Ceddia 2026 studied the optimal training routines implied by Banister and Busso's models and explored modifications of the general form (his Eq. 17)

    \[R^{(C)}_t = \sum_{s=0}^{t-1}M_F(w_s)\,e^{-(t-s)/\tau_F(w_s)} - k_G\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}w_s\,e^{-(t-s)/\tau_G(w_s)},\]

    in which the fitness gain may show diminishing returns in the training impulse and either time constant may depend on the load of the session that produced it. The fatigue impulse stays linear in \(w_s\): Ceddia declines to modify it, as that "would amount to redefining the training impulse itself". Of the forms he explored, his Fig. 10 and conclusion section single out the combination of a power-law diminishing return in the fitness magnitude (his Eq. 20) and time constants that grow linearly with the impulse (his Eq. 30 with \(m = 1\)). BIRM's ceddia core implements this form:

    \[M_F(w) = k_F\,\frac{(1 + w)^{1 - k_D} - 1}{1 - k_D}, \qquad \tau_F(w) = \tau_F + \tau_{F1} w, \qquad \tau_G(w) = \tau_G + \tau_{G1} w,\]

    with \(k_D \ge 0\) and \(\tau_{F1}, \tau_{G1} \ge 0\). In Ceddia's notation, \(k_1 = k_F\), \(k_2 = k_G\), \(k_3 = k_D\), \(\tau_{11} = \tau_F\), \(\tau_{12} = \tau_{F1}\), \(\tau_{21} = \tau_G\) and \(\tau_{22} = \tau_{G1}\). \(M_F\) has slope \(k_F\) at \(w = 0\), tends to \(k_F \log(1 + w)\) as \(k_D \to 1\), and reduces to Banister's \(k_F w\) at \(k_D = 0\). Setting \(k_D = \tau_{F1} = \tau_{G1} = 0\) recovers the Banister model exactly.

    Because \(M_F\) depends on \(1 + w\), the curvature of the diminishing return depends on the units of the load. The decay-time slopes are made unit-free by sampling the fractional lengthening \(\rho\) of each time constant after a session of typical load \(\bar w\) (the mean load over training days): \(\tau_{F1} = \rho_F \tau_F/\bar w\) and \(\tau_{G1} = \rho_G \tau_G/\bar w\). Under the informed parameterization, \(\rho_F = \rho_G + \lambda_\rho\) with \(\lambda_\rho \ge 0\). Together with \(\tau_F > \tau_G + 1\), this keeps \(\tau_F(w) > \tau_G(w) + 1\) at every load.

    Ceddia's Section 5.1 notes that such parameters are unlikely to be inferable from weekly or fortnightly performance tests. BIRM's priors on \(k_D\), \(\rho_G\) and \(\lambda_\rho\) are therefore HalfNormal and shrink toward the Banister model; densely observed metrics such as HRV are what give the data a chance to move them.

    Example: HRV and speed

    \(Y^{(1)}_t\) and \(Y^{(2)}_t\) are HRV and speed, respectively. \(X^{(1)}_t\) is the training load \(w_t\) to represent a possible daily HRV suppression effect \(\beta^{(1)}_t\), and \(X^{(2)}_t\) contains outdoor temperature (to adjust for heat) and an indicator of whether each run was performed indoors on a treadmill, giving speed two exogenous predictors. HRV is observed near daily, so we include a \(u^{(1)}_t\) term. speed is more sparsely observed; no \(u\) is warranted. Both channels use the identity link \(l^{(1)}(x) = l^{(2)}(x) = x\), and we set \(\gamma^{(1)} \equiv 1\) so the scale of the IR model is pinned to the more densely observed channel HRV. The shared impulse-response model is the Ceddia variant, whose optimal training sequence sheds fatigue while still training. The joint model is therefore

    \[ Y^{(1)}_t = \alpha^{(1)} + R^{(C)}_t + X^{(1)}_t\beta^{(1)} + u^{(1)}_t + \epsilon^{(1)}_t\]
    \[ Y^{(2)}_t = \alpha^{(2)} + \gamma^{(2)} R^{(C)}_t + X^{(2)}_t\beta^{(2)} + \epsilon^{(2)}_t\]

    You would define this model in BIRM like so:

    model = IRModel(
    load=Load("w"),  # the training-load driver w_t
    core="ceddia",
    metrics=(
        Metric("hrv", exog=("w",), ar1=True),  # hrv suppression + drift (u)
        Metric("speed", exog=("temp", "indoor")),  # heat + treadmill effects
    ),
)
